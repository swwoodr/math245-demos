📐 Hard springs, soft springs, and amplitude-dependent frequency ▼

A linear spring (Hooke's law, $\beta = 0$) has the same period $T_0 = 2\pi/\sqrt{\alpha}$ at every amplitude. Real springs stiffen or weaken when stretched far, which the cubic term models:

Hard spring ($\beta > 0$): the restoring force grows faster than linearly, so large oscillations are faster — the period shrinks as amplitude grows.

Soft spring ($\beta < 0$): the force weakens at large $x$, so large oscillations are slower. Beyond $x_s = \sqrt{-\alpha/\beta}$ the force points outward: $(\pm x_s, 0)$ are saddle points, and a mass with too much energy escapes. The simple pendulum is a soft spring, since $\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$.

With no damping, energy is conserved: $E = \tfrac12 (x')^2 + \tfrac12\alpha x^2 + \tfrac14\beta x^4$. The faint curves in the phase plane are level sets of $E$, and the period at amplitude $A$ is

$$T(A) = 4\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{\alpha + \tfrac12\beta A^2\,(1 + \sin^2\varphi)}} \;\approx\; T_0\left(1 - \frac{3\beta A^2}{8\alpha}\right).$$

Like the pendulum, the Duffing equation has no elementary closed-form solution, so it is solved numerically (here with RK4; in MATLAB, write it as a first-order system and use ode45). Compare the Simple Pendulum and Pendulum Phase Plane demos.
