📐 Where the laws come from ▼

Aimed fire (each shooter can target individual enemies): a side's loss rate depends only on how many enemy shooters there are, so $x' = -a\,y$, $y' = -b\,x$. Dividing, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{b\,x}{a\,y}$, which separates to $a\,y\,dy = b\,x\,dx$ and integrates to

$$b\,x^2 - a\,y^2 = b\,x_0^2 - a\,y_0^2 = K.$$

Blue wins exactly when $K > 0$. Fighting strength grows with the square of numbers: doubling a force is worth quadrupling its effectiveness, which is why concentrating force matters.

Area fire (firing into a region, e.g. artillery): losses also depend on how densely the target is packed, so $x' = -a\,x\,y$, $y' = -b\,x\,y$. Now $dy/dx = b/a$ and $b\,x - a\,y$ is conserved — strength is only linear in numbers.

Guerrilla vs. conventional: the hidden guerrilla force $x$ takes area fire, $x' = -a\,x\,y$, while the exposed conventional force takes aimed fire, $y' = -b\,x$. This gives $a\,y^2 - 2b\,x = \text{const}$ (a parabolic law), and the conventional side needs a large numerical advantage to win.

Each is a nonlinear or linear autonomous system, so the phase-plane ideas from Block III apply directly: the conserved quantity traces out the trajectory, and the line or curve where it equals zero separates the two outcomes. Reinforcements (constant $P$, $Q$ added to $x'$ and $y'$) break the conservation law, so the winning regions are no longer drawn.
