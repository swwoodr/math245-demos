📐 Why linearization works — and when it doesn't ▼

Near an equilibrium $\mathbf{x}^*$, Taylor's theorem gives $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^*) + J(\mathbf{x}^*)(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^*) + \cdots$, and $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$. Dropping the higher-order terms leaves the linear system $\mathbf{u}' = J\mathbf{u}$ with $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^*$ and

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x & \partial f/\partial y \\ \partial g/\partial x & \partial g/\partial y \end{pmatrix}_{\mathbf{x}^*}.$$

If the equilibrium is hyperbolic (no eigenvalue of $J$ has zero real part), the nonlinear system behaves like its linearization near $\mathbf{x}^*$: saddles stay saddles, spirals stay spirals, and stability is the same. Shrink the zoom window and watch the two portraits become indistinguishable.

If the linearization is a center (purely imaginary eigenvalues) or has a zero eigenvalue, the neglected terms decide the outcome, and linearization is inconclusive — try the "Linearization fails" system. (When eigenvalues are real and equal, stability is still decided, but the nonlinear system may be a node or a spiral.)
